\[

=	& a^2-b^2
\end{array}

\[
\begin{array}{rl}
	& (a+b)^2=(a+b)(a+b) \\
=	& (a+b)a+(a+b)b \\
=	& a^2+ab+ab+b^2 \\

\end{array}
\]

\end{array}
\]

\section*{2.5}

\subsection*{Polynomial Long Division}


We consider a polynomial $f(x)$. It can be equally written as \[ a_nx^n+a_n-1x^{n-1}+...+a_0x^0 \] where $n$ is the degree of $f(x)$. We suppose 
that $f(x)$ is being divided by some $d(x)$. This can be expanded as \[ b_mx^m+b_mx^{m-1}+...+b_0x^0 \] where $m$ is the degree of $d(x)$. Define 
the set of polynomials \[ p_1(x),p_2(x),...,p_j(x) \] in the following way: \[ p_1(x)=f(x)-d(x)(\frac{a_nx^n}{b_mx^m}) \]
\[ p_2(x)=p_1(x)-d(x)(\frac{t}{b_mx^m}) \] where $t$ is the leading term of $p_1(x)$; and, in general (when $i>1$),
 \[ p_i(x)=p_{i-1}(x)-d(x)(\frac{t_{i-1}}{b_mx^m}) \]
 where 
$t_i$ is the leading term of $p_{i-1}$, $t_0$ is $a_nx^n$, and $p_0-f(x)$, and $i>1$. 
The last term, $p_j(x)$, is such that the degree of $p_j(x)$ is less than $m$. We are to show that 
\[ \frac{f(x)}{d(x)}=\frac{t_1}{b_mx^m}+\frac{t_2}{b_mx^m}+...+\frac{t_{j-1}}{b_mx^m}+\frac{p_j(x)}{d(x)} \] that is,
\[ f(x)=\frac{t_1d(x)}{b_mx^m}+\frac{t_2d(x)}{b_mx^m}+...+\frac{t_{j-1}d(x)}{b_mx^m}+p_j(x) \]
Because we defined every $p_i(x)$ such that $p_i(x)=p_{i-1}(x)-\frac{t_{i-1}d(x)}{b_mx^m}$, it follows that $p_j(x)+\frac{t_{j-1}d(x)}{b_mx^m}=p_{j-1}$, 
and, in general, $p_i(x)+\frac{t_{i-1}d(x)}{b_mx^m}=p_{i-1}$. But this is the process being done in the above sum, but for every $i$. Thus,
\[ \frac{t_1d(x)}{b_mx^m}+\frac{t_2d(x)}{b_mx^m}+...+\frac{t_{j-1}d(x)}{b_mx^m}+p_j(x)=p_1(x)+\frac{t_1d(x)}{b_mx^m}=f(x) \] thus completing the proof.

\subsection*{Remainder Theorem}


We consider a polynomial $f(x)$ being divided by $x-k$. We are to show that the remainder is $r=f(k)$. Well, by the above result:
\[ f(x)=\frac{t_1d(x)}{x}+\frac{t_2d(x)}{x}+...+\frac{t_{j-1}d(x)}{x}+p_j(x) \] where $j$ is defined from the above algorithm. Because we are dividing by a 
linear polynomial, the remainder will be a constant term. In particular, this constant will be equal to $p_j(x)$. Now, consider the following sequence 
$g_1,g_2,...,g_n$ of terms:
\[ g_1=a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\frac{a_nx^n}{x}k \]
\[ g_2=a_{n-2}x^{n-2}+\frac{g_1}{x}k \]
and, in general:
\[ g_i=a_{n-i}x^{n-i}+\frac{g_{i-1}}{x}k \]
where $n$ is the degree of $f(x)$ and every $a_i$ is defined from the polynomial long division algorithm. 
Because, as stated, the remainder of 
$\frac{f(x)}{x-k}$ is $p_j(x)$, we can see that $g_j=p_j(x)$. Now, $g_1$ consists of adding one $a_{n-1}$, and $a_n$ $k$ times. $g_2$ consists of adding 
one $a_{n-2}$, $a_{n-1}$ $k$ times, and $a_n$ $k^2$ times. In general, $g_i$ consists of $a_{n-i}$ 1 time, $a_{n-(i-1)}$ $k$ times, and in general, 
every $a_m$ $k^{m-(n-i)}$ times, all multiplied by $x^{n-i}$. Of course, the last term, $g_n$, is such that $x^{n-i}=1$, because $i=n$. Thus, we can see that 
the last term, which is equal to $p_j(x)$, the remainder, is just $a_nk^n+a_{n-1}k^{n-1}+...+a_0k^0$, that is, $f(k)$, thus completing the proof. 

\subsection*{Synthetic Division}


Here, we are trying to show that the last result obtained in synthetic division is the remainder, while the other numbers on the bottom line 
are the respective coefficients of the quotient, if $f(x)$ is being divided by some $x-k$. By the Remainder Theorem, the last result that 
the last number is the remainder is obtained, because this last number is just $f(k)$, which has been shown to be the remainder of $\frac{f(x)}{x-k}$. 
As for the other numbers, we already know that the coefficients of the quotient are just all of the $\frac{t_i}{x}$. 
We can see that the first result of synthetic 
substitution is just $a_nx^{n-1}$, which happens to be equal to $t_0$, that is, $\frac{a_nx^n}{x}$. In the second case, we can see that adding 
$a_n\times k$ to $a_{n-1}$ to get $(a_{n-1}+a_nk)x^{n-2}$ is just the same as $\frac{t_1}{x}$, that is, 
\[ \frac{a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\frac{a_nkx^n}{x}}{x}=(a_{n-1}+a_nk)x^{n-2} \] and, in general, every $i$th result from synthetic substitution is just 
adding the result from the previous substitution, call it $s$, multiplying it by $k$, and adding this to $a_{n-i}$. Thus every result is just 
\[ (sk+a_{n-i})x^{n-(i+1)} \]
where $i\ge 0$, and the previous term when $i=0$ is just $1$. This is, of course, is equal to
\[ \frac{t_{i+1}}{x} \] that is, \[ (sk+a_{n-i})x^{n-(i+1)} \] 
if we let $s$ be the first term of $p_{i-1}$ (because we only add anything to $a_{n-i}$ once because 
this is a linear term we are dividing by).  

\subsection*{Factor Theorem}


We want to show that a polynomial $f(x)$ has a factor $x-k$ iff $f(k)=0$. On the one hand, suppose $f(x)$ has a factor $x-k$. Hence,
\[
\begin{array}{rl}
	& f(x)=(x-k)y \\
\rightarrow & \frac{f(x)}{x-k}=y
\end{array}
\]
meaning $\frac{f(x)}{x-k}$ has a remainder of $0$, with a quotient of $y$. On the other hand, suppose that 
$f(k)=0$. Well, by the Remainder Theorem, $\frac{f(x)}{x-k}$ has a remainder of $f(k)$. Because we assumed $f(k)=0$, this means $\frac{f(x)}{x-k}$ has a 
remainder of $0$, thus meaning,
\[ \frac{f(x)}{x-k}=y \] that is, \[ f(x)=(x-k)y \] meaning $x-k$ is a factor of $f(x)$.

\subsection*{Lemma 1}
Prove that if $a|b$ and $a|c$, then $a|b+c$.\newline
Suppose $a|b,a|c$. Thus:
\[ b+c=ak+aj=a(k+j) \] for some $k,j$, completing the proof.

\subsection*{Euclid's Lemma (for review)}

We wish to prove
\[ \textrm{if } a,n\textrm{ are coprime, and } mn=ab\textrm{ then } n|b \]
We make a complete induction assumption: for all numbers $<ab$, the hypothesis holds for every $m,n$.\newline
Case 1: $a=n$:\newline This means $n=1$ (because $\textrm{gcd}(a,n)=1$) and thus $n|b$.\newline
Case 2: $n<a$:\newline 
\[ mn=ab \]
\[ mn-nb=ab-nb \]
\[ n(m-b)=b(a-n) \]
We know that $\textrm{gcd}(n,a-n)=1$ by Lemma 1 and the assumed coprimality of $a,n$. 
Because $\textrm{gcd}(n,a-n)=1$, by the induction assumption, $n|b$.\newline
Case 3: $n>a$:\newline
\[ mn=ab \]
\[ mn-am=ab-am \]
\[ m(n-a)=a(b-m) \]
By Lemma 1 and the assumed coprimality of $a,n$, $\textrm{gcd}(a,n-a)=1$. Thus $a|m$ and $aj=m$ for some $j$:
\[ aj=m \]
\[ ajn=mn \]
\[ ajn=mn=ab \]
\[ ajn=ab \]
\[ jn=b \]
that is, $n|b$.

\subsection*{Lemma 2}
Prove that if $a,b$ are coprimes, then $a,b^n$ are coprimes for every $n$.\newline
Suppose that $a,b^n$ are not coprime, that is, for some $c$:
\[ cd=a\wedge kc=b^n \]
meaning:
\[ kc=b\times b^{n-1} \]
Because $a,b$ are coprimes, and $c\mid a$, $c,b$ are coprimes (becuase, if some $qr=c$ and $qo=b$ for some $q\ne 1$ 
, $qrd=a$, contrary to the assumption 
of coprimality between $a,b$). Thus, $c\mid b^{n-1}$ by Euclid's Lemma, meaning, for some h:
\[ hc=b\times b^{n-2} \] 
Again, because $a,b$ are coprimes, and $c\mid a$, $c,b$ are coprimes. We then repeat this process, but it must end eventually, as $n$ is finite. 
Thus, we will eventually end up with:
\[ cs=b \]
meaning $c|b$, contrary to the assumption that $a,b$ were coprimes.

\subsection*{Rational Roots Theorem}
Consider some polynomial
\[ a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+...+a_1x+a_0=0 \]
where every $a_i$ is an integer. We are to show that every rational zero of this polynomial is of the form:
\[ \frac{p}{q} \] where $p$ is a factor of $a_0$, and $q$ is a factor of $a_n$. First, we substitute $\dfrac{p}{q}$ in for $x$:
\[ a_n\frac{p^n}{q^n}+a_{n-1}\frac{p^{n-1}}{q^{n-1}}+...+a_1\frac{p}{q}+a_0=0 \]
\[ a_np^n+a_{n-1}qp^{n-1}+...+a_1pq^{n-1}+a_0q^n=0\textrm{ Multiply both sides by $q^n$} \]
\[ a_np^n+a_{n-1}qp^{n-1}+...+a_1pq^{n-1}=-a_0q^n \]
\[ p(a_np^{n-1}+a_{n-1}qp^{n-2}+...+a_1q^{n-1})=-a_0q^n \]
Note that $\dfrac{p}{q}$ is assumed to be in simplest terms, meaning $\textrm{gcd}(p,q)=1$, and consequently, by Lemma 2, 
$\textrm{gcd}(p,q^n)=1$, and thus $\textrm{gcd}(p,-q^n)=1$ (for, if some $ab=p$ and $ak=-q^n$ then $-ka=q^n$, meaning both $p$ and $q^n$ share a factor 
of $a$, contrary to the assumption of their coprimality). 
Thus, by Euclid's Lemma, $p|a_0$. On the other hand, we have:
\[ a_{n-1}qp^{n-1}+...+a_1pq^{n-1}+a_0q^n=-a_np^n \] 
\[ q(a_{n-1}p^{n-1}+...+a_1pq^{n-2}+a_0q^{n-1})=-a_np^n \]
and with similar reasoning to the first case, we can show that $q|a_n$.
